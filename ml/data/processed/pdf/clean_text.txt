7	INTRODUCTION.
7	The following manual had its inception while the author
7	was teaching signs to a class of hearing teachers. After
7	the signs had been shown, a written description was furnished as a guide for reference. The success of the plan
7	.made it evident that such a vocabulary of signs would be
7	a help to those who were anxious to learn the sign language
7	I and to others who felt the occasional need of reference to
7	some standard to refresh their memory or add to the signs
7	already known. The interest shown in acquiring the lan guage of the deaf, by both oral and manual teachers, was
7	convincing proof that there are still a great many teachers
7	vho realize (whether they use signs or not in teaching)
7	that a knowledge of this means of communicating with the
7	deaf helps them understand their pupils and brings them
7	closer to the hearts of their little charges, besides giving
7	them a better knowledge of the deaf outside of the school
7	room.
7	The work is not presented with the idea that persons
7	unfamiliar with the deaf or their language can take it up
7	and therefrom master the art of communicating in pantomime and understand its peculiarities. But it is believed that those who have had some experience with the
7	deaf and have opportunities to see the signs made will find
7	4 it easy to follow the instructions given. As with all other
7	languages, so with this language, ease and familiarity in
8	its use and the mastery of its idiom come only by long pra
8	tice and association with the deaf.
8	In spite of all that has been said and done, by zealou
8	advocates of mistaken theories, to relegate the sign la uag
8	to the past, it is very much a live language. Its plac n th
8	lives, and as a factor in the happiness of, the deaf c no
8	be overlooked or denied. Its necessity in their social inter
8	course with one another, in the pulpit, and on the plarm
8	is fully recognized by all those who understand it, and who!
8	realize and appreciate the needs of the deaf, regard!
8	whether they favor one or another method for their instruction.
8	But the tendency to discard signs more and more nom
8	the school room has resulted in its neglect so far as regards
8	teaching it correctly to pupils. By the haphazard way in
8	which they pick it up from the playground it is not acquired
8	by the rising generation in that purity and perfection at-j

8	Another hope of the author is that it will preserve this
8	expressive language, to which the deaf owe so much, in its

10	The sign language used by the deaf in the United States
10	and Canada we get from the French. It was introduced
10	here by the Rev. Thomas H. Gallaudet when he founded
10	the first permanent school for the deaf in America at Hartford, Connecticut, in 1817. It has received many additions,
10	changes, and modifications under the influence of a natural
10	development and under different conditions of use, and the
10	language we use at present is materially different from that
10	now used in France.
10	The French owed the origin of the language to the Abbe
10	de PEpee, who first undertook the instruction of the deaf
10	in France and established a school for their education at
10	Paris in 1759. ffis vocabulary was extremely limited and
10	it was later enlarged by the Abbe Sicard who succeeded him
10	at the head' of the school. It was from Sicard and his pupil
10	Laurent Clerc that Mr. Gallaudet learned the language.
10	When Rev. Thomas H. Gallaudet went to England in
10	1815 to inform himself as to methods of instructing the
10	deaf, he found the work in that country under a monopoly
10	in the hands of the Braidwood family. They refused to
10	allow him access to their secrets or to give him any assistance except under conditions with which he could not comply. He met the Abbe Sicard in London and was by him
10	cordially invited to visit the school at Paris. There he was
10	shown every courtesy and spent several months studying
11	methods and learning the sign language. Returning to
11	America he brought with him Laurent Clerc, a graduate of
11	the Paris school and then a teacher in his alma mater.
11	When the school at Hartford was opened, Clerc was employed as an instructor, teaching the sign language to other
11	instructors, and thus the so-called "French method" was
11	introduced into this country.

13	Other omissions are those of the prepositions and the
13	articles. Also in asking a question the briefest sign, coupled
13	with a look of inquiry, or a simple sign in reply to a question,
13	may express the idea contained in a whole sentence. It is,
13	for the most part, this tendency to eliminate, coupled with

13	The haphazard, slipshod manner of using signs is to be
13	strongly condemned and the English order should be
13	followed as nearly as possible.
13	The mastery of the sign language consists not so much
13	in the number of signs one may know as in the cleverness
13	with which those he does know are used. Many different
13	ideas can be expressed with a few signs coupled with natural
13	gestures. Many ideas having no sign of their own may
13	readily be communicated by signs to define them. Indeed,
13	many words must be signed in this way, -which somewhat

14	In connection with its incompleteness and imperfection
14	when compared with written or spoken language, it should
14	be remembered that the sign language was originally designed as a means of educating young deaf children and
14	the vocabulary was therefore limited. Its usefulness has
14	gone far beyond this first purpose, and incomplete and imperfect though it may be, it is, in the hands of its masters,
15	a most beautiful and expressive language, for which, in their
15	intercourse with each other and as a means of easily and
15	quickly reaching the minds of the deaf, neither nature nor
15	art has given them a satisfactory substitute.
15	It is impossible for those who do not understand it to
15	comprehend its possibilities with the deaf, its powerful
15	influence on the moral and social happiness of those deprived of hearing, and its wonderful power of carrying
15	thought to intellects which would otherwise be in perpetual
15	darkness. Nor can they appreciate the hold it has upon
15	the deaf. So long as there are two deaf people upon the
15	face of the earth and they get together, so long will signs
15	be in use.
16	EXPLANATORY.
16	In describing the position assumed to make the sign it is
16	given as it would be in making the sign by itself. When a
16	number of signs are made in succession, as in a continued
16	narrative, they fall into the next position from the last
16	without any stop, the movement of the arms and hands
16	being practically continuous. The sign is made, usually
16	in front of the body in an easy position, the hands, unless
16	otherwise specified, being held about the waist line.
16	In order to save repetition and ambiguity in description,
16	certain positions of the hands used in making the manual
16	alphabet are taken as basic positions. (See frontispiece.)
16	These are referred to as the "A" hand, the "B" hand, and
16	so on, the positions being those of the hand in making those
16	letters. In the "&" hand it should be noted that the last
16	position of the hand in making the "&" is meant, and not
16	its movement part. Besides the letter positions there are
16	several others as follows
16	The "extended" or the "open hand." Hold the hand
16	open, the fingers extended and close together, with the
16	thumb extended. It might be described as the position
16	of the hand when one offers it to shake.
16	The "closed 0' hand." Make "O" with the thumb
16	and forefinger while all the other fingers are closed against
16	the palm as in the fist. The "extended '0' hand." Make "O" with the thumb
17	and forefinger as above but extend all the other fingers
17	straight out.
17	The "bent hand." Bend the fingers in toward the palm
17	but not touching it. The fingers assume about the position
17	they have in making "C," but the thumb is extended.
17	The "5 hand." Hold the hand open, the fingers ex- tended and separated, as in showing the number 5.
17	In looking for any word disregard its grammatical form
17	and look for the word which most nearly seems to express
17	the idea.

18	To Be, to Exist.—Hold the forefinger of the right "G"
18	hand pointing upward, against the mouth; move the whole
18	hand, finger still held upright, forward straight out from
18	the mouth; then bring the "A" hands together, the end
18	of the right thumb resting on the nail of the left thumb,
18	and move both hands this way forward. (Note: This is
18	the present and acceptable way to make this sign though
18	formerly there were other signs. These are not here given, as it seems unnecessary.) The usual way is to make simply
18	the first part of the sign with the forefinger, and then finish
18	with "now" or "past" to indicate tense, and "many" to
18	indicate plural.
18	To Be, infinitive and imperative.—Hold the right "G"
18	hand straight out in front from the side, the "G" up;
18	crook the forefinger and move the hand toward the left
18	side horizontally in front of the body.
18	To Be, auxiliary of the passive voice.—Press the forefinger
18	of the right "G" hand, pointing upward, against the mouth;
18	then bring both open hands, palm toward palm, pointing
18	upward, to the side of the face and throw each back over
18	the corresponding shoulders, letting the thumbs strike
18	against and come to rest on the shoulders.
18	Can, expressing possibility, power, etc. —Hold the "S"
18	hands out in front, elbows against sides, and let the hands
18	drop a little way with a jerk.

22	hand pointing straight out and elevated to a level with

22	The tense signs are as follows: For the present, same as "now," i. e., Hold the open or bent hands, in front, from
22	the sides, palms up, and drop them both at the same time
22	with a slight jerking motion. The past is indicated by

22	rarely given unless essential to make it specific. Usually

70	Note.—In general it may be laid down as a rule that
70	the sign for an action should bear some resemblance to
70	the natural movement.
70	Go, Leave 'present place, Depart.—Make motion with finger or hand away from the body. Also, hold left open hand
70	in front, palm toward breast; rest right hand on back of
70	left hand, remove, and motion outward. The latter is
70	more correctly for "send."

70	palms down, out toward the left side, one farther toward
70	the left than the other, somewhat "tandem;" draw them
70	back toward the right side of the body and a little up,
70	bending the fingers down from the knuckles.

70	toward the left side, palms toward each other, left hand

106	ADJECTIVES AND ABSTRACT NOUNS.
106	Note: In the sign there is no distinguishing difference, as a rule, between the adjective and the abstract noun.
106	Whether one or the other is meant is determined by the
106	context or the circumstances under which it is used. Comparison is indicated by following the sign for the adjective
106	by a sign indicating the degree, without any pause, thus:
106	For the comparative ("-er"), draw the "A" hand out
106	toward the right side in front, raising it with a slight
106	jerking motion to the height of the shoulder. For the
106	superlative ("-est") make the same motion but carry the
106	hand higher to a level just above the head. For example,
106	in making the sign for "better" the end of the open hand
106	is placed against the mouth and the sign for "good" is
106	made, but before quite finishing it the "A" hand is brought
106	up the side as indicated so that there is no stop made,
106	the whole movement appearing as one sign. To make
106	"than," bring the right hand from the sign of the adjective
106	and degree quickly downward, palm down, past the left
106	open hand, held vertically edgewise, pointing outward.
106	Good.—Place the end of the palm against the mouth;
106	then bring it down against the open left hand so the back
106	of the right hand rests in the palm of the left. In common
106	use the latter part of the sign is omitted and the hand is
106	simply thrown forward from the mouth.
106	Bad.—Same position of the hand as in "good;" in bringing the hand away draw it slightly toward the right, turn
106	it palm down and throw the hand downward.

191	COUNTING.
191	I to 10.—Begin by holding up the forefinger for 1; then
191	two fingers for 2; for 3 bring up the thumb with the two
191	fingers; for 4 draw back the thumb against the palm and
191	hold up the four fingers, and for 5 the fingers and thumb.
191	For 6, while the other fingers are extended, draw down the
191	end of the little finger against the end of the thumb; for
191	7 draw down the third finger same, and for 8 and 9 the
191	middle and forefinger respectively. Note that only one
191	finger touches the thumb at a time, all the remaining
191	fingers being extended. For 10, hold up the thumb of
191	the "A" hand and shake it from side to side with a twisting motion from the wrist but not moving the arm, or,
191	extending the thumb of the " A" hand toward the left,
191	give it a twisting jerk toward the left (not moving the arm)
191	until it points upward.
191	II to 20. —Make 10 as described in last line and then
191	add all the other digits up to 9. In common practice this
191	is shortened and instead of making the full sign for 10 the
191	forefinger is extended from under the thumb of "A"
191	hand as if forcibly released for 11, and the same with the
191	two fingers for 12; for 13 the two fingers and thumb are thrown out straight with the twisting motion of 10; for
191	14 the fingers are thrown out and the thumb drawn down
191	quickly against the palm; for 5 the whole hand is thrown
191	open; and for 16, 17, 18, and 19 the hand is closed as in
191	"A" and the fingers are thrown out into the positions
192	for the figures, the hand at first always being in position
192	of "A." For 20 the position of the right hand is as in " G, " except that the thumb and forefinger have been
192	drawn farther apart and the palm is down; move the hand
192	to the right with a slight jerk and close the ends of the
192	thumb and forefinger. In common practice the thumb
192	and forefinger in position as above are simply snapped
192	together.
192	21 to 30.—In the twenties, begin the motion with the
192	"L" hand, palm outward; throw the hand toward the
192	right with a jerk and then add the numbers 1 to 9. The
192	jerk is not essential but is advisable as it adds distinctness
192	to the figures. This is especially true of figures above 50.
192	For 30 make 3; then jerk hand to right and make
192	cipher with the ends of the thumb and fingers brought
192	to a point.
192	31 to 40.—Make 3; jerk hand to the right and add the
192	figures as above. For 40 make 4; then jerk to right and
192	make cipher for "0."
192	41 to 50.—Make 4; then jerk hand to right and add
192	figures as above. For 50 make 5 and then "0," the jerk
192	always being part of the movement.

193	Arithmetic.—Hold the "V" hands pointing upward, the
193	palms toward self; throw the right hand toward the left,
193	and the left toward the right so they meet and cross, the
193	back of the right "V" passing against the palm of the
193	left "V." Repeat the motion several times.
193	Multiply.—Made the same as for " arithmetic, " but the
193	motion made but once instead of being repeated several
193	times.
193	Add.—Hold the left "&" hand pointing up and rather
193	low; upon its end place the end of the right "&" hand
193	pointing down; move both hands upward, lifting the right
193	hand and striking it against the left several times as they
193	rise.
193	Subtract.—Holding the open left hand with palm toward
193	self, strike the ends of the fingers of the right bent hand
193	against its palm and drop the right hand down away from
193	the left, as if taking something away from it.

194	"G" hands trace in the air the two right angles placed
194	at either side of the dividend.

194	forefinger with the end of the forefinger of the right "G"
194	hand, thus indicating the right angle placed under the
194	dividend.
